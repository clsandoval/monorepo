# 2.08 — Panic Eviction: Under Pressure, Buffer Dumps Aggressively — Unit "Forgets" Rapidly

**Aspect:** 2.08 — Panic eviction as a stress response: when units face extreme pressure (combat proximity, overload, multiple simultaneous signals), they dump context window contents aggressively, losing potentially valuable data in the panic
**Wave:** 2 (Core Mechanic Variations)
**Dependencies:** 2.01 (Fixed-Slot Buffer), 2.02c (Weight-Aware Eviction Policies), 2.07 (Automatic Eviction Rules)

---

## The Design Question

Standard eviction is orderly. A signal arrives, the buffer is full, one entry gets removed according to the configured policy. It is bookkeeping — tidy, predictable, player-controlled. Panic eviction asks: **what happens when the system breaks down?**

In real agentic systems, there is a qualitative difference between "one new message arrived and the context window is full" and "fourteen messages arrived in two ticks, three enemies just appeared in perception range, and the unit's relay channel is flooding." The former is routine memory management. The latter is a system under duress — and systems under duress behave differently than systems at rest.

This is **"The Amygdala Mechanic."** When a unit enters a panic state, its eviction policy stops being the careful, player-configured rulebook and becomes a brutal survival reflex: dump everything that is not immediately relevant to staying alive. The unit forgets its mission, forgets the terrain it mapped, forgets the command it received three ticks ago — all in service of processing the immediate threat. It narrows its attention to a survival aperture.

The player consequence: your beautifully tuned information architecture can shatter under pressure. A scout that was holding compressed threat data for three allied strikers panics when two enemies appear simultaneously, dumps the compressed data, and now only knows about the enemies directly in front of it. The strikers never get the intel. The flank collapses. Not because of a design flaw in the eviction rules — because the scout's buffer was overwhelmed faster than the rules could process cleanly.

## Mechanical Specification

### Panic Threshold

Each unit has a **panic threshold** — a numeric value representing the maximum incoming signal load the unit can process calmly in a single tick. When the number of simultaneous events exceeds this threshold, the unit enters **panic mode** for the current tick.

Events that count toward the panic threshold:
- Each new signal arriving from a subscribed channel (+1 per signal)
- Each new enemy entering perception range (+2 per enemy — threats are more alarming than data)
- Each new observation generated by the unit's own perception (+1 per observation)
- Each allied unit destroyed within perception range (+3 — catastrophic event)
- Buffer already at 100% capacity at tick start (+1 baseline stress)

**Proposed panic thresholds by unit type:**

| Unit | Buffer Size | Panic Threshold | Rationale |
|------|-------------|-----------------|-----------|
| Scout | 6 | 4 | Small buffer, easily overwhelmed — panics fast |
| Striker | 8 | 6 | Combat-oriented, slightly more composed under fire |
| Relay | 12 | 10 | Built to handle signal volume — high panic resistance |
| Specialist | 10 | 5 | Deep focus, brittle under pressure — panics when disrupted |
| Command | 14 | 8 | Large buffer but manages many channels — moderate resilience |

The Relay's high threshold makes it the calm anchor in a storm — a design that reinforces its role as infrastructure. The Specialist's low threshold relative to buffer size creates a fragile-genius archetype: powerful when protected, catastrophic when exposed.

### Panic Eviction Behavior

When a unit enters panic mode, its configured eviction rules are **suspended** for that tick. Instead, the engine applies a hardcoded survival eviction cascade:

1. **Purge all entries older than 3 ticks** — stale data is the first to go
2. **Purge all entries of type `terrain`** — spatial memory is sacrificed for survival awareness
3. **Purge all entries of type `relay` or `compressed`** — secondhand information is less trusted than direct observation under stress
4. **If buffer is still over 50% full, purge all entries except `threat` type** — pure survival tunnel vision

After the cascade, the unit processes the current tick's incoming events using whatever buffer space remains. If the buffer is *still* full after the panic purge (because all remaining entries are threat-type), the unit enters **context overload** — the standard 1-tick stun.

This means panic can *cause* overload. A unit can panic-dump its entire context, then still overload because the incoming threat volume exceeds even the cleared buffer. The double penalty (panic data loss + stun) makes it devastating.

### Player Countermeasures

Panic is not inevitable. The player has tools to prevent it:

- **Channel filtering** — Reduce the number of channels a unit listens to. Fewer subscriptions = fewer simultaneous signals = lower panic risk. But also less information.
- **Relay compression** — A Relay's `compress` skill can consolidate multiple signals into one before forwarding. Three scout reports become one compressed summary. The downstream striker receives 1 signal instead of 3 — panic threshold stays low.
- **Positioning** — Keep fragile units (Specialists) away from the front line. If enemies never enter perception range, the +2 per enemy penalty never triggers.
- **Hook throttling** — Configure hooks to fire every N ticks instead of every tick. A scout that reports every 2 ticks generates half the signal volume.
- **Buffer headroom** — Keep buffers below capacity. A unit entering a tick at 50% buffer has room to absorb a spike. A unit entering at 100% is one signal away from panic.
- **The "Stress Inoculation" rule** (advanced) — Using aspect 2.07's eviction rule grammar, the player can write pre-emptive eviction rules that trim the buffer *before* panic threshold is reached. Example: `WHEN buffer.fill > 75% AND enemies_in_range > 1: EVICT oldest WHERE type == "terrain"`. This is the player manually writing what the panic cascade does automatically — but on their terms, preserving higher-value data.

### Visual and Audio Language

**Pre-panic warning (threshold at 75%):**
The unit's context bar — the row of horizontal pips below its sprite — begins to flicker. Not the steady glow of a filling buffer, but an irregular strobe, like a fluorescent light about to die. The unit's sprite develops a subtle vibration — not the dramatic jitter of overload, but a nervous tremor. A low electrical hum fades in, just at the edge of audibility.

**Panic trigger:**
The context bar flashes white for a single frame, then slots begin winking out in rapid succession — blink, blink, blink, blink — each pip collapsing from its assigned color (blue for terrain, amber for observations, cyan for relay data) to dark grey. The eviction cascade is visible as a waterfall of dying lights. The unit's sprite flashes a pale yellow (not the red of combat, not the blue of signals — a sickly fight-or-flight yellow). A sharp, descending synthesizer tone plays — the sound of a hard drive being wiped, compressed into half a second. Think the degauss sound from old CRT monitors.

**Post-panic state:**
The unit's context bar shows only the surviving entries — usually just 1-3 bright red pips (threat data) in a field of empty dark slots. The unit looks lobotomized. Its sprite has a faint residual tremor for the next 2 ticks, and its perception radius pulses with a slightly tighter, more focused circle — visually communicating tunnel vision.

**Inspector view of a panic tick:**
In the debrief timeline, panic ticks are marked with a jagged yellow border around the tick pip (standard ticks have smooth borders). Clicking a panic tick opens a special "Panic Cascade" view: a vertical timeline showing each eviction in order, with the purged entry's content fading to grey and sliding off-screen to the right. At the bottom, a counter reads "7 entries lost" in yellow text. The decision trace for that tick shows a truncated rule evaluation — "PANIC: standard rules suspended, survival cascade active."

## The Strategic Depth of Forgetting

### Information as Liability

Panic eviction inverts the standard assumption that more information is always better. A unit subscribed to five channels has more data — and a much higher panic risk. The player who wires every scout to every striker creates a fragile web: one multi-enemy encounter cascades through the entire network, panicking every unit simultaneously.

This creates a genuine design tension: **breadth of awareness vs. resilience under pressure**. The player who gives each striker only one scout's channel has units that know less but crack less. The player who builds a fully connected mesh has units that know everything until the moment they know nothing.

### Panic Chains

The most devastating emergent behavior: **panic cascading through a network**. Scout A panics and dumps its buffer, but one of its hooks fires *during the panic tick* (hooks fire before eviction in the tick resolution order), sending a burst of "I'm panicking" signals across its channels. Relay B receives Scout A's panic burst plus its normal signal load — the combined volume pushes Relay B past its panic threshold. Relay B panics and dumps, and *its* hooks fire, flooding the command channel. Command C, listening to three relays, receives three simultaneous panic bursts and panics itself.

In five ticks, the entire network has forgotten everything. The player watches their carefully orchestrated army revert to disconnected, terrified individuals stumbling through fog of war — each unit knowing only what its own sensors can see, right now, this tick.

This is the "Neural Cascade Failure" — and it is the penalty for building deep, interconnected architectures without panic mitigation. The player who has experienced this will never again build a network without considering failure modes. That is the teaching moment.

### Enemy Exploitation

Smart enemy AI can deliberately trigger panic cascades:

- **"The Flood"** — Enemy spawner releases 4-5 weak units simultaneously into a scout's perception range. The scout panics, dumps intel about the *real* threat (a striker flanking from the east), and the flood units scatter. The scout recovers but has lost the critical data.
- **"The Noise Bomb"** — An enemy specialist emits a burst of fake signals on common channel names, overwhelming relay buffers. No physical threat — pure information warfare.
- **"The Ambush"** — Enemies remain hidden until multiple units are in position, then reveal simultaneously. Every player unit in the area receives multiple +2 enemy-detection events in one tick.

## Strengths

1. **Viscerally legible failure.** The panic cascade is visible, audible, and comprehensible. The player can *see* their network forgetting. This is the "information overload must be viscerally legible" constraint made into a mechanic.
2. **Teaches real engineering principles.** Graceful degradation, circuit breakers, backpressure — these are real distributed systems concepts. A player who learns to build panic-resistant networks has learned something transferable.
3. **Creates dramatic moments.** The sealed watch phase becomes genuinely tense when you know your front-line scouts are operating near their panic thresholds. The moment a cascade triggers is a gut-punch — watching your army forget in real-time.
4. **Rewards architectural thinking.** You cannot solve panic by micro-managing individual units. You solve it by designing resilient architectures: bottleneck relays, throttled channels, buffer headroom budgets. System-level thinking.
5. **Makes the Relay essential.** The Relay's high panic threshold and compression skills make it the circuit breaker that prevents cascades. Without Relays, networks are fragile. This reinforces the unit roster's design intent.

## Weaknesses

1. **Potentially frustrating for new players.** A beginner who does not understand panic thresholds will experience their units "randomly forgetting things" and blame the game. The tutorial must teach panic before players encounter it in high-stakes missions.
2. **Reduces player agency during panic.** The player configured eviction rules for a reason — having them suspended during panic removes player control. Some players will feel cheated. Counter-argument: the player's *pre-panic* decisions (architecture, filtering, positioning) are where agency lives.
3. **Tuning difficulty.** Panic thresholds are numbers that must be carefully balanced. Too low and panic is constant and annoying. Too high and it never triggers, wasting the mechanic. Scenario-specific tuning will be required.
4. **Complexity budget.** This is yet another system layered on top of eviction rules, context config, and overload stuns. The cognitive load is cumulative. Each new mechanic must justify its complexity cost.
5. **Cascade failures can feel unfair.** When one scout panicking causes the entire army to forget, the player may feel the punishment is disproportionate. The teaching must emphasize that cascades are preventable, not random.

## Interaction Effects

### With 2.07 (Automatic Eviction Rules)
Player-authored eviction rules are the primary countermeasure against panic. The "Stress Inoculation" pattern — pre-emptive eviction rules that fire before panic threshold is reached — turns eviction rules from a memory management tool into a resilience engineering tool. The two systems have a productive tension: eviction rules prevent panic, but eviction rule slots compete with channel subscriptions for context config slots.

### With Sealed Watch (No Pause, No Tools)
Panic cascades during sealed watch are *irreversible*. The player cannot pause, cannot intervene, cannot adjust. They watch the cascade unfold and must wait for the Inspector phase to understand what happened. This amplifies the emotional impact — which is the entire point of the sealed watch design.

### With Inspector (Decision Trace)
The Inspector's panic cascade view is where learning happens. The player scrubs to the panic tick, sees the cascade waterfall, traces the chain of events: "Scout A received 5 signals in one tick... panic threshold was 4... terrain and relay data purged... hook fired on channel `recon-net`... Relay B received 3 simultaneous signals from that hook plus 2 from other scouts... panic threshold was 10 but it was already at 8 incoming..." The Inspector turns a frustrating loss into an engineering postmortem.

### With Production Queue (Factory Model)
After experiencing panic cascades, the player may redesign their production queue to include more Relays — trading combat power for network resilience. A "2 Relays per 3 Strikers" ratio becomes a rule of thumb. This is the factory-level strategic consequence of a tactical mechanic.

### With One-Shot-One-Kill Combat
Panic is especially punishing in a one-shot-one-kill system. A stunned unit (from post-panic overload) is a dead unit if an enemy striker is adjacent. There is no HP buffer to absorb the mistake. Panic does not just lose information — it kills units.

### With Signal Latency (1 Tick Per Hop)
Multi-hop architectures (Scout → Relay → Relay → Command) create natural panic mitigation: signals arrive staggered across ticks rather than simultaneously. But this means deeper architectures are both louder (EM emissions) and more panic-resistant — a meaningful tradeoff.

## Comparable Games and Media

### Darkest Dungeon — Stress and Afflictions
Darkest Dungeon's stress system is the closest analog. When a hero's stress reaches 100, they develop an affliction that overrides player control — a Paranoid hero refuses healing, a Hopeless hero passes turns, an Abusive hero insults allies and raises their stress. The parallel to panic eviction is direct: a system-level break that suspends normal behavior and imposes survival-mode actions the player did not choose. Darkest Dungeon's insight: afflictions feel fair because the player can see stress building and has tools to manage it (camping skills, stress-healing abilities, retreating from fights). Panic eviction must similarly telegraph its approach and offer preventive tools.

### FTL: Faster Than Light — System Cascades
FTL's fire and breach mechanics create cascade failures: a fire in the oxygen room reduces O2, which weakens crew, who fight the fire more slowly, which lets it spread. The parallel to panic chains is structural — one local failure propagating through interconnected systems. FTL teaches that cascades are the most memorable moments in the game: the stories players tell are always "and then the fire reached the weapons bay and everything went wrong." Panic cascades should similarly be the stories Robot Uprising players tell.

### XCOM — Panic Mechanic
XCOM's panic mechanic is a direct precedent: soldiers under fire can panic, losing their turn or shooting randomly. It is widely considered one of XCOM's most frustrating mechanics because it feels random and uncontrollable. The lesson: panic mechanics must feel *preventable*. If the player believes they could have avoided the panic through better planning, they blame themselves. If they believe it was random chance, they blame the game. Robot Uprising's panic thresholds (numeric, visible, configurable) should feel more deterministic than XCOM's hidden dice rolls.

### Real-World Distributed Systems — Circuit Breakers
The circuit breaker pattern in microservices architecture is the direct engineering analog. When a service detects it is being overwhelmed (too many requests, too many timeouts), it "trips" the circuit breaker and starts rejecting all requests immediately rather than slowly degrading. Netflix's Hystrix library popularized this pattern. The parallel: a Relay configured with pre-panic eviction rules is implementing a circuit breaker. The player is literally learning distributed systems resilience engineering through gameplay.

## Player Journeys

### Journey: Marcus, 34, Software Engineer

**Context:** Mission 5, first factory mission. Marcus has completed the tutorial missions and understands basic context windows and eviction. He built a mesh network — every scout reports to every striker via a shared `threat-intel` channel. No Relays. He is proud of the simplicity.

**Minute 0:00 — The Plan**
Marcus's workbench shows three blueprints: Scout (2 hook slots, both broadcasting on `threat-intel`), Striker (listening to `threat-intel`, rule: "if threat in range, engage"), and a production queue of Scout, Scout, Striker, Striker, Striker. The board preview shows his base in the southwest corner, enemy spawner in the northeast. Jungle terrain tiles scattered across the middle. He clicks EXECUTE.

**Minute 0:15 — Calm Before**
Sealed watch. Tick 1-8 unfold smoothly. Two scouts fan out northeast. Three strikers hold a line behind them. Scouts detect individual enemies — one or two per tick — and broadcast on `threat-intel`. Each striker's context bar fills steadily: blue for terrain observations, amber for their own perception, cyan for relay data from the scouts. The bars are at about 60% by tick 8. Green cell flashes show `threat-intel` signals flowing. Marcus nods. Working as designed.

**Minute 0:35 — The Flood**
Tick 9. The enemy spawner activates and releases four enemy scouts simultaneously into the eastern quadrant. Scout Alpha, positioned at E5, detects all four. Its context bar — six tiny pips — was already showing 4 occupied (amber, amber, blue, cyan). Now: +4 enemy detection events (+2 each = 8 panic points), plus its own new observation (+1), plus the incoming signal from Scout Beta on `threat-intel` reporting the same enemies (+1). Total incoming load: 10. Panic threshold: 4.

Scout Alpha's pips strobe white. Blink-blink-blink-blink — four pips collapse from their colors to dark grey in rapid succession. Only two red pips remain: the two nearest enemies. A pale yellow flash washes over Scout Alpha's sprite. The descending synthesizer whine cuts through the ambient battlefield hum. Marcus's hands tighten on the mouse.

**Minute 0:45 — The Chain**
But Scout Alpha's hooks fired *before* the panic cascade. Two `threat-intel` signals are already in flight — one for each hook slot. These signals, plus Scout Beta's own reports, arrive at all three Strikers simultaneously at Tick 10. Striker One, at C3, receives: 2 signals from Alpha, 1 signal from Beta, plus its own 2 perception observations (it can see one enemy from its narrow range). Total incoming: 5. Panic threshold: 6. It holds. The context bar flickers but stabilizes.

Striker Two, at D4, is closer to the action. It receives: 2 signals from Alpha, 1 from Beta, plus 3 perception observations (two enemies are in its narrow range, +2 each = 4 panic points from enemies alone, plus 1 from its own observation). Total incoming to the panic counter: 4 (enemies) + 3 (signals) + 1 (observation) = 8. Panic threshold: 6. Panic triggers.

Striker Two's context bar waterfalls — terrain data gone, the compressed intel from the last five ticks gone, the command directive about holding the line gone. Only the two red threat pips survive. Striker Two now knows there are enemies nearby. It has forgotten *which* enemies, *where* the flanking threat is, *what* it was supposed to be doing. Its rule evaluates: "if threat in range, engage." It engages the nearest enemy — the decoy scout directly in front of it — while the real enemy striker approaches from the south, unseen, unmarked, forgotten.

**Minute 1:10 — The Post-Mortem**
The battle ends in a loss. Marcus enters the Inspector. He scrubs to Tick 9 and clicks Scout Alpha. The Panic Cascade view opens: a vertical timeline of seven events arriving in one tick, each rendered as a colored bar slamming into a threshold line. Below the threshold line, the eviction waterfall: terrain (blue, fading to grey, sliding right), relay data (cyan, fading, sliding), observations (amber, fading). The counter at the bottom: "4 entries lost." He scrubs to Tick 10 and clicks Striker Two. Same cascade, different data. He traces the chain: Alpha's panic hook → signal on `threat-intel` → Striker Two overwhelmed.

Marcus stares at the channel map. Every unit connected to every other unit on one channel. He opens the blueprint editor in his mind. He needs a Relay. He needs to break the mesh into segments. He needs circuit breakers.

**UI Annotations:**
- **Panic cascade view (Inspector):** Full-width panel replacing the standard decision trace. Yellow jagged border on the tick pip in the timeline. Vertical waterfall showing each purged entry with type icon, source label, and age counter, each fading from its type color to grey and sliding off-screen right. Total count in yellow monospace at bottom.
- **Panic flash (Sealed Watch):** Unit sprite flashes pale yellow (#FFFACD) for 200ms. Context bar pips collapse sequentially at 80ms intervals. Descending synth tone: 800Hz → 200Hz over 400ms.
- **Pre-panic flicker:** When incoming load exceeds 75% of panic threshold, context bar pips strobe at irregular intervals (randomized 100-300ms on/off). Subtle sprite vibration: 1px random offset per frame.

### Journey: Anya, 22, Competitive Card Game Player

**Context:** Mission 7, second command agent mission. Anya has experienced panic cascades before and developed a personal doctrine: "Never more than 2 channels per unit. Always one Relay per 2 combat units." She is building a resilient architecture.

**Minute 0:00 — The Architecture**
Anya's workbench shows five blueprints. Two Scouts, each on their own channel (`east-eye`, `west-eye`). One Relay, listening to both scout channels, compressing signals, outputting on `filtered-intel`. Two Strikers, both listening to `filtered-intel` only. The Relay's context config shows an eviction rule she wrote: `WHEN buffer.fill > 66%: EVICT oldest WHERE type == "terrain"`. This is her stress inoculation — pre-emptive trimming to keep the Relay's buffer below panic threshold.

She also has a Command unit listening to `filtered-intel` with a rule: `WHEN buffer.count("threat") > 3: reassign nearest striker to highest-threat position`. The Command's eviction rules: `WHEN buffer.fill > 50%: EVICT oldest WHERE type == "observation"`. Aggressive pre-trimming. The Command should never panic — it has a threshold of 8 and only listens to one channel.

Production queue: Scout, Relay, Striker, Scout, Striker. She hits EXECUTE.

**Minute 0:20 — Stress Test**
Ticks 1-12 are textbook. Scouts fan east and west. Relay sits at center, compressing and forwarding. Strikers advance on `filtered-intel` guidance. Anya watches the Relay's context bar with particular attention: it fills to 8/12, her eviction rule fires (a brief amber flash as a terrain entry is ejected), it drops to 7/12. The rule fires again two ticks later. The Relay is breathing — filling and trimming rhythmically. No panic.

At Tick 13, the enemy launches a two-front assault: three enemies from the east, two from the west. Scout East detects three (+6 panic points from enemies, +1 observation). Total: 7. Threshold: 4. Scout East panics. Its context bar waterfalls — the familiar yellow flash and descending tone. But Anya designed for this. Scout East's panic hook fires one signal on `east-eye`. Just one signal.

The Relay receives: 1 signal from `east-eye`, 1 signal from `west-eye` (Scout West detected 2 enemies but its load was only 5 — threshold of 4 — so Scout West *also* panics, sending 1 signal). Relay incoming: 2 signals. Plus its own context overhead. Total: 3. Threshold: 10. The Relay does not panic. It compresses both panic signals into a single entry: "Threat: multi-front contact, east (3) and west (2)." Forwards on `filtered-intel`.

Each Striker receives 1 compressed signal. No panic. They act on good intel despite both scouts having panicked. Anya's architecture held.

**Minute 0:45 — The Weak Point**
But the scouts are compromised. Post-panic, Scout East has only 2 entries: the 2 nearest enemies. It has forgotten the terrain, forgotten the third enemy (the one at the edge of perception), forgotten the relay data about the western front. Its next tick, it reports only what it can see — incomplete data. Scout West is similarly lobotomized.

The Relay compresses this incomplete data faithfully. The Strikers receive accurate-but-incomplete intel. The third eastern enemy — the one Scout East forgot — flanks Striker One. Red flash. One-shot-one-kill. Striker One is eliminated.

Anya swears. The architecture held against the cascade, but the *quality* of post-panic information degraded. She needs her scouts to recover faster. She opens her mental notebook: "Scout eviction rules. After panic, prioritize re-scanning. Maybe a `WHEN buffer.count("threat") < enemies_in_range` condition to force re-observation." The game is teaching her about recovery protocols.

**Minute 1:15 — Debrief**
In the Inspector, Anya opens the context window chart for Scout East: a sparkline of buffer utilization over all ticks. It shows a steady green line at 66% from Ticks 1-12, then a jagged plunge to 33% at Tick 13 (panic), a yellow recovery zone from Ticks 14-16 as the buffer refills, and a return to green by Tick 17. The dip is the wound. She traces the missing third enemy through the decision chain: "Scout East lost Entry #4 (enemy at G6) during panic... never re-acquired... Relay never compressed it... Striker One never received it... Striker One moved to F5 assuming clear eastern flank... Enemy at G6 advanced to F5 at Tick 16... elimination."

She saves a mental note: "I need a post-panic re-scan behavior. Maybe a rule: `WHEN buffer.count("threat") == 0 AND enemies_in_range > 0: patrol (re-scan)`."

**UI Annotations:**
- **Relay buffer breathing (Sealed Watch):** Context bar pips show rhythmic eviction — one pip dims and a new pip brightens in the same tick, creating a conveyor-belt visual. Amber micro-flash (50ms) on the evicted pip. Distinct from panic cascade (no yellow flash, no descending tone).
- **Compressed signal delivery:** When the Relay forwards a compressed signal, the signal delivery flash is green but with a subtle compression artifact — the green is slightly pixelated or dithered, visually distinct from raw signal delivery. Communicates "this is processed data, not raw."
- **Post-panic sparkline (Inspector):** Context window chart uses three colors — green (healthy, <66%), amber (stressed, 66-90%), red (critical, >90%). Panic events are marked with a yellow lightning bolt icon on the sparkline. The post-panic dip shows as a sharp V-shaped valley with yellow fill.

Fifteen seconds: A zoomed-in isometric view of a Scout surrounded by jungle tiles. Its six context bar pips glow steadily — amber, amber, blue, cyan, cyan, empty. Calm ambient hum. ## Open Questions

1. **Should panic threshold be player-configurable?** A context config option "panic resistance" that lets the player trade buffer slots for higher panic thresholds. More resilient but less capacity. Currently proposed as fixed per unit type, but configurable thresholds add another meaningful decision.

2. **Partial panic?** Instead of binary (calm/panic), a graduated stress system where increasing pressure causes increasingly aggressive eviction. At 75% of threshold, evict oldest terrain. At 100%, full cascade. At 150%, evict everything except the single highest-priority threat. This adds granularity but complexity.

3. **Panic recovery speed.** How quickly does a unit return to normal after panic? Currently instantaneous — the panic cascade fires in one tick and normal rules resume the next tick. Should there be a "shell-shocked" debuff that keeps eviction rules suspended for 1-2 additional ticks?

4. **Panic immunity for Relays?** Given the Relay's role as network backbone, should Relays be immune to panic entirely? Or does Relay panic (which requires 10+ simultaneous events) serve as the "everything has gone wrong" signal — a legitimate failure state for truly catastrophic scenarios?

5. **Hook behavior during panic.** Currently proposed: hooks fire before the panic cascade. Should hooks be suppressed during panic instead? Suppressing hooks prevents cascade propagation but also prevents the scout from sending its last useful signal. Allowing hooks enables cascades but preserves the unit's communication duties. Both options have strong arguments.
